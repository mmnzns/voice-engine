# Speed on real models, 2026-10-29

Numbers from this Mac for the bars the apps asked for (spec 9.4 and 9.5), and the downloader run once against the
real Hugging Face. Everything here was measured by the examples in this repository; no transcript, prompt or reply
was printed or kept, only times, sizes and counts.

## The machine

| | |
|---|---|
| Chip | Apple M3 Pro (`sysctl -n machdep.cpu.brand_string`), 12 logical cores (6 performance and 6 efficiency) |
| Memory | 18 GB (`hw.memsize` 19,327,352,832 bytes) |
| System | macOS 27.2 (build 26B123), on AC power |
| Toolchain | rustc 1.98.1, `--release` for everything measured |
| Load | Not a quiet machine: `uptime` showed load averages of 2.7 to 6.3 while the speech runs were going (the Stats menu bar app at 16 to 32 percent of one core and a browser were the top processes); it was 0.7 to 3.0 during the AI runs. Everything below was run with those programs open. |

One model at a time everywhere: each example loads one model, measures it, drops it, then loads the next; two model
processes were never running together.

### Bars, plainly

* **Turbo on Metal, about 25x: missed, 19.9x.** The thread count does not explain it: 4, 6, 8 (default) and 12 threads

* **Parakeet Ultra, 0.43 s for 29 s: missed.** 0.49 s with the default 9 threads, 0.44 s with Parlato's 12 (0.01 s over
  the floor, inside the run-to-run spread of 0.44 to 0.57 s seen in the four invocations). The thread count explains
  most of the gap. Other counts: 6 threads 0.50 s, 8 threads 0.48 s, 10 threads 0.46 s.
* **Parakeet Ultra, about 50x on a Windows processor:** not comparable, this is a Mac processor (here 59.7x with the
  default threads and 65.2x with 12, for what it is worth).
* **Whisper Base, 0.50 s for 29 s: missed, 0.79 s with the default threads and 0.72 s with 6.** Threads help a little but
  nothing on the processor gets near 0.50 s: 4 threads 0.84 s, 8 threads 0.79 s, 12 threads 0.84 s. With the graphics
  card (Metal, default 8 threads) the same model decodes in 0.36 s (0.36 to 0.36), 80.9x. The floor is for the
  processor, so this is a result to look at, not a bar met.

None of the engine's settings were changed to chase a bar.

## Why the bars were missed

Follow-up measurement, same Mac, same 29 s clip and the same method as above (one load, one warm-up decode, 3 timed
decodes, median of the 3), by `cargo run --release -p voice-engine-stt --example bench_timing` (new: `-- base` or
`-- turbo` for one model; one model is loaded at a time). The question: `Timing::Segments` and `Timing::Words` both turn
on whisper.cpp's token timestamps, to give words in both modes, and Parlato's dictation does not need them. Do they
cost the time? Four ways, each with the engine's default threads (Base on the processor 9, Turbo on Metal 8) and with 6:

1. **engine**: `WhisperEngine`, `Timing::Segments`, as it is.
2. **talaan**: whisper-rs called directly with exactly the settings of `voice_engine_stt::transcribe` (Talaan's current
   chunk function, the reference the real tests compare `Timing::Segments` against: no token timestamps).
3. **tokens on**: whisper-rs directly with the engine's settings (Talaan's, plus `set_token_timestamps(true)`).
4. **tokens off**: the same with `set_token_timestamps(false)`.

The engine with the token timestamps off was also run once (a local patch to `whisper_engine.rs`, not committed: the
flag read from an environment variable; its words came back empty, which shows the patch was on).

Median decode time in seconds per invocation of the example. Whisper Base on the processor: invocations 1 to 3 were run
within minutes of each other with the same programs open as above (invocation 1 while a `tar` and Spotlight were each
using a core, load average 6.5), and "quiet" was run later at load average 2.3 (Spotlight still on one core):

| Whisper Base, cpu | Threads | Inv. 1 | Inv. 2 | Inv. 3 | Quiet |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| engine | 9 (default) | 0.84 | 0.78 | 0.78 | 0.78 |
| talaan | 9 | 0.81 | 0.77 | 0.76 | 0.76 |
| tokens on | 9 | 0.82 | 0.78 | 0.79 | 0.77 |
| tokens off | 9 | 0.80 | 0.76 | 0.76 | 0.76 |
| engine | 6 | 0.87 | 0.71 | 0.71 | 0.72 |
| talaan | 6 | 0.85 | 0.70 | 0.71 | 0.70 |
| tokens on | 6 | 0.87 | 0.71 | 0.71 | 0.72 |
| tokens off | 6 | 0.86 | 0.70 | 0.70 | 0.70 |

The run-to-run range inside an invocation was 0.00 to 0.06 s. Engine with the token timestamps off (patched): 0.85 s with
9 threads and 0.87 s with 6, in a stretch where the unpatched engine gave 0.84 and 0.87 (invocation 1).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| engine | 8 (default) | 1.44 | 1.46 | 1.46 |

Engine with the token timestamps off (patched): 1.43 s with 8 threads and 1.43 s with 6. Every run decoded the same
speech (7 segments and 94 words, also with the timestamps off).

What it says:

* **The token timestamps cost 1 to 4 percent on Whisper Base** (tokens on to tokens off in the same invocation: 0.01 to
  0.03 s of 0.76 to 0.82 s with 9 threads, 1.1 to 2.8 percent of 0.70 to 0.87 s with 6; the largest saving in any
  invocation was 3.8 percent) **and about 1 percent on Turbo** (0.02 s of 1.45 s). The hypothesis is not confirmed, and
  the 15 percent threshold the option was conditioned on is far away. No engine code was changed: no `word_times` option
  was added.
* **The engine adds little over Talaan's own function:** 0.01 to 0.03 s on Base with the default threads (1 to 4
  percent, 0.00 to 0.02 s with 6) and 0.00 to 0.03 s on Turbo: the token reading, `words::clean` and the resume
  bookkeeping.
* **The reference itself misses the bars.** Talaan's own function, the code the apps run today, takes 0.70 to 0.71 s for
  Base with 6 threads (bar 0.50 s) and 1.44 to 1.45 s for Turbo on Metal (20.0 to 20.1x, bar about 25x). So the gap is
  not something the engine put there.
* Turbo's thread count does not matter (8 and 6 are the same to 0.01 s): the card is the limit, as before.

Also tried, with no effect on Base:

* **A different CPU baseline.** The engine builds whisper.cpp with `GGML_NATIVE=OFF` (`.cargo/config.toml`), which on
  Apple silicon leaves out the newest instructions of the chip. A scratch build with `GGML_NATIVE=ON` (`-mcpu=native`,
  in a separate target folder, not committed): engine 0.79 and 0.78 s with 9 threads, 0.71 and 0.74 s with 6 (talaan 0.76
  and 0.77, 0.70 and 0.73): the same. Whisper Base's time is not in the instruction set the library is compiled for.
* **A quiet machine.** The "quiet" column above (load average 2.3) is the same as the busy ones. The programs open during
  the earlier runs are not what keeps Base above 0.50 s.
* Another build with `GGML_BLAS=OFF` (no Accelerate) did not compile here and was not pursued.

What the real cause might be (none of these was verified here, and Parlato's code was not read, so its settings are not
known to this repository):

* **The audio.** The clip is a text-to-speech voice reading 94 words in 29 s with 0.3 s between sentences: a decode of
  about as many tokens as speech can have. A real dictation has pauses and fewer tokens per second, and a decode step
  costs time for each token; Parlato's floor of 0.50 s may come from a recording with less speech in it.
* **The model file.** Base here is the 148 MB `ggml-base.bin` (f16, sha256 in the downloader section above); Turbo here is
  the compressed one (`ggml-large-v3-turbo-q5_0.bin`). A different quantisation of either runs at another speed, on Metal
  too. Both bars may have been measured with another file.
* **The library version.** This build is whisper-rs 0.16.0 with whisper-rs-sys 0.15.0, which is whisper.cpp 1.8.3. A
  build with another whisper.cpp (the Metal and CPU kernels change between versions) can be faster or slower.
* **The six efficiency cores.** This is an M3 Pro with 6 performance and 6 efficiency cores. The thread sweep (4 threads
  0.84 s, 6 0.72, 8 0.79, 12 0.84) has its best at the number of performance cores, which is what you see when work
  split evenly across threads has to wait for the slow ones; it explains why 6 beats 9, not the gap to 0.50 s.

The way to settle it is to run the engine on the same audio and the same model file as Parlato: ask the Parlato chat for
the recording (or one of its 29 s test clips), the hash of its Base and Turbo files and the whisper-rs version in its
`Cargo.lock`, then run `bench_timing` on that audio. Until then the Base and Turbo bars stay missed, with the engine no
slower than Talaan's own code by more than a few percent.

## The local AI (a 50-word cleanup)

How it was measured: `LlmClient` with the real helper `target/release/voice-engine-llm`, models from the shared folder.
The request: the system prompt "fix punctuation and remove filler words, reply with the cleaned text only", thinking off,
temperature 0, context 4096, `max_tokens` 160, and a fixed messy dictation of exactly 50 words (two different
sentences with spoken fillers, no punctuation, no capitals; 90 tokens in with the template). An exact repeat of one
25-word sentence was tried first and rejected: the models answered it with the sentence once (22 to 20 tokens out), which
is not a 50-word cleanup. One `load` (the helper's own load time), one warm-up request, then 3 timed requests. Reply
time is the wall time from sending the request to the full reply. Prompt time is until the helper reported the whole
prompt read; tokens per second is output tokens over (reply time minus prompt time). All three replies of each model
had the same token count and none was truncated.

| Granite 4.2 3B | metal | 1.20 s | 0.82 s (0.82 to 0.85) | 90 | 34 | 43.8 |
| Qwen3.5 9B (once) | metal | 3.02 s | 2.34 s (2.32 to 2.36) | 90 | 46 | 20.2 |

The prompt takes 0.04 to 0.06 s of each reply. The loads are the first load of the helper process; a second load of the

**The processor rows (`gpu: false`).** The two `cpu` rows were measured later the same day, after `gpu: false` was
made to mean the processor on the Mac: the helper puts no layer on Metal, keeps the KV cache and the big batches off
it too, and says `cpu`. Same request and method (one `load`, one warm-up, 3 timed requests; `bench_llm` with its

(With the repeated sentence that was tried first, Granite's two replies were the same text.)

## Fillers

Spec 9.4: does the model keep "um", "uh" and "like" in the transcript, with and without `keep_fillers`? Measured by
`crates/stt/tests/real_fillers.rs` (`cargo test --release -p voice-engine-stt --test real_fillers -- --ignored
--test-threads=1 --nocapture`, one model at a time), on the same Mac as above, with `whisper-turbo` (Metal, default
threads) and `parakeet-ultra` (processor) from Talaan's folder.

How: 7 sentences, 9 spoken fillers in each recording (um 3, uh 2, hmm 2, like 2, in varied places: start, middle,
end of a sentence, and one sentence with two), said by three macOS `say` voices (Samantha US, Daniel UK, Karen AU:
recordings of 32.5, 34.0 and 33.4 s, 0.4 s between sentences), 27 spoken fillers per model and option. `like` appears
only as a filler in the sentences. `Timing::Words`, language auto, no vocabulary. A filler in the transcript is a word
that, with the punctuation around it trimmed, is `um`, `umm`, `uh`, `uhh`, `hmm` or `like` (whole word, any case). Per
voice, a count is capped at the 9 spoken (an extra one is not a kept one); the test only prints filler tokens and
counts, never the transcript. The test also checks that five ordinary words of the recording came back, so a count of
0 cannot mean "nothing was decoded". The same counts came out in two separate runs.

Fillers found per recording, out of 9 spoken (kinds in brackets: um, uh, hmm, like):

| Model | Voice | keep_fillers false | keep_fillers true |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whisper Large Turbo | Samantha | 8 (3, 2, 1, 2) | 9 (3, 2, 2, 2) |
| Whisper Large Turbo | Daniel | 9 (5, 2, 0, 2) | 9 (5, 2, 0, 2) |
| Whisper Large Turbo | Karen | 7 (3, 2, 0, 2) | 7 (3, 2, 0, 2) |
| **Whisper Large Turbo, all** | | **24 of 27 (89%)** | **25 of 27 (93%)** |
| Parakeet Ultra | Samantha | 7 (3, 2, 0, 2) | 7 (3, 2, 0, 2) |
| Parakeet Ultra | Daniel | 9 (5, 2, 0, 2) | 9 (5, 2, 0, 2) |
| Parakeet Ultra | Karen | 8 (4, 2, 0, 2) | 8 (4, 2, 0, 2) |
| **Parakeet Ultra, all** | | **24 of 27 (89%)** | **24 of 27 (89%)** |

The rule (plan, Task 2): `Some(true)` when with the option at least 80% of the spoken fillers appear and that is more
than without it, or the model keeps them either way; `Some(false)` when fewer than 80% appear even with the option.

* **`whisper-turbo`: `keeps_fillers = Some(true)`.** It keeps them without the option too (89%); the option adds one
  "hmm" (Samantha, 8 to 9) and nothing else. Whether that one token is the prompt's doing is doubtful: with the
  filler prompt replaced by a filler-free sentence ("Let me think. Okay. Here is what I am thinking.") the counts were
  exactly the same (checked once, then put back). What changed the decode was having a prompt at all.
* **`parakeet-ultra`: `keeps_fillers = Some(true)`.** The option does nothing for it (the identical counts, as the
  engine says), and it keeps the fillers as heard, 89% either way.

Where the two models differ from "kept them all": `um`, `uh` and `like` came back every time, in both models, all voices
and both options (21 of 21 each). `hmm` is the weak one: Whisper wrote it twice out of 6 (both with the option, both
Samantha), Parakeet never wrote "hmm". In Daniel's and Karen's recordings the spoken "hmm" came out as "um" in the
transcript (Daniel: um 5 for 3 spoken; Karen with Parakeet: um 4), which the count above takes as a filler that was
kept. Counted kind by kind instead (an "um" in place of a "hmm" is not a hit), Whisper gets 22 of 27 (81%) without and 23
of 27 (85%) with the option, and Parakeet 21 of 27 (78%) either way, below the 80% line. So for Parakeet the verdict
depends on how an "hmm" heard as "um" is scored; for `um`, `uh` and `like` (what spec 9.4 names) it is 100%.

### Can text-to-speech fillers be trusted?

Not fully. A `say` voice reads "um" as a clean, stressed syllable with a short pause around it. A real speaker's "um"
is often short, quiet, half swallowed, run into the next word, and sometimes not a word at all ("mm", a breath). Models
were trained on real speech and may drop such sounds more often than the clean ones tested here, so this likely
overstates how well each model keeps real fillers; it is evidence that they can write "um", "uh" and "like" when they
are said clearly, and that Whisper's option made almost no difference to clear ones. The test also has few data points
(27 spoken fillers per model, three voices, one recording each, no noise), and "hmm" is the least reliable of the
sounds in the recording: `say` voices differ a lot in how they say it, and the models turned it into "um" or nothing.
An app that depends on this flag for real speech should check it on a real recording of its own people, particularly
for Whisper without the option (where a real "um" may be dropped; the test cannot show that).

The flag for every other speech model stays `None`: they were not measured.

## What is NOT measured
